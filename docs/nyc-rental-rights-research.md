# NYC rental rights: language and connection design

Research checked October 8, 2026. This note separates current law and agency guidance from recommended Prime America policy. It is a research input for broker/counsel review, not a certification of compliance. No application code changed.

## Lawful sources of income and vouchers

NYC Administrative Code § 8-107(5) prohibits covered housing providers and real estate professionals from discriminating because of lawful income sources, including discriminatory advertising and withholding available housing. Source-of-income protection covers more than Section 8: CityFHEPS, other rental assistance and non-employment income matter too. A landlord's instruction does not make a discriminatory response appropriate for a broker. [NYC Human Rights Law](https://www.nyc.gov/site/cchr/law/chapter-1.page).

NYC HRA identifies refusal to show apartments, unequal access to applications, extra cash/guarantor demands, and minimum employment income or credit scores where a voucher pays the full rent as warning signs. [HRA's renter guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hra/help/income-discrimination.page).

CCHR's published tenant-advocate FAQ distinguishes full and partial rent coverage. When the program would approve the rent and determines the applicant's share according to income, insufficient income and guarantor requirements cannot be used to reject the applicant. If the voucher pays 100%, rejection based on credit is inappropriate; when the applicant pays a portion, credit may be considered case by case. A minimum score may violate city law. This is not a guarantee that every voucher application or property will receive program approval. [CCHR FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/cchr/downloads/pdf/materials/FairHouse_FAQTenant-2020Eng.pdf).

**Applicability requires review:** city pages are inconsistent on small-owner thresholds: HRA describes three-unit buildings, while CCHR's older FAQ describes six-unit ownership and exceptions. These summaries should not become an automated exemption rule. Check current codified provisions, ownership portfolio, owner occupancy, advertising, rent regulation, and other applicable laws with counsel. [HRA guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hra/help/income-discrimination.page), [CCHR FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/cchr/downloads/pdf/materials/FairHouse_FAQTenant-2020Eng.pdf).

**Material statewide litigation:** on March 5, 2026, the Third Department affirmed a declaration that Executive Law § 296(5)(a)(1) is facially unconstitutional insofar as it requires acceptance of Section 8 vouchers, reasoning that program participation requires warrantless inspections. That decision addresses the state provision, not a declaration invalidating NYC's separate ordinance. [Commons West opinion](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/3dseries/2026/2026_01253.htm). On May 21 the Court of Appeals denied a stay motion **as unnecessary**, citing CPLR 5519(a)(1); that is not a denial on the merits or a simple statement that no stay applies. [Motion decision](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/current/motions/2026/2026_68687.shtml). The court's calendar schedules the merits appeal, APL 2026-35, for October 20, 2026, after this research date. Do not publish a simplistic statewide repeal claim or treat older DHR guidance as resolving the litigation. [Official October case issues](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/calendar/2026/Monthly/Oct26CaseIssues.pdf).

**Proposed brokerage policy:** welcome all lawful income sources throughout our service. Never offer a “vouchers accepted” filter that makes ordinary listings appear unavailable to assisted renters. Use “Ask about rental assistance and application requirements,” with optional program details only when helpful; never make disclosure necessary to browse or request a showing. Avoid “working vouchers only,” “no programs,” “employment required,” and unreviewed universal “40× rent / 700 credit” rules. Queue inquiries consistently and audit delays, not only explicit rejections. These workflow recommendations go beyond merely quoting the law.

## NYC broker fees: FARE Act

DCWP's FAQ, updated June 2, 2026, says the law took effect June 11, 2025 and is enforced by DCWP. A landlord's agent, including a broker publishing a listing with the landlord's permission, cannot impose or collect a rental broker fee from the tenant. A rental cannot be conditioned on hiring a particular broker or dual agent. Renters may independently choose to hire and pay their own broker. All tenant-payable fees must be conspicuously disclosed in advertisements/listings; before lease signing, landlords/agents must provide a signed itemized written fee disclosure, give the tenant a copy, and retain it for three years. Older contractual obligations can require individual review. [DCWP current FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/FAQ-Broker-Fees.page).

**Litigation limit:** the current official agency page supports implementing the law as effective and enforced. This research did not obtain the latest FARE appellate opinion directly from the court; it does not certify that every challenge has ended. Counsel should review the appellate/docket record and any pre-effective-date agreements before advising on exceptions. Do not tell consumers that a pending challenge suspends ordinary fee duties.

**Proposed interface:** identify “Listing agent — represents the landlord” and “No landlord-agent broker fee charged to the renter” when the responsible team verifies that statement. Show every separately verified permitted fee, its recipient, basis and refundability. Do not infer “no fees” from a missing broker-fee field. Direct contact or a showing request must not silently hire a tenant's agent.

## Application charges and security deposits

For covered residential applications, RPL § 238-a ordinarily bars landlord/lessor/sublessor/grantor application-processing charges and limits combined background/credit reimbursement to the lesser of actual cost or $20. It requires a report and receipt/invoice and waiver when the applicant supplies a qualifying report from the prior 30 days. The statute contains specific co-op owner/shareholder exceptions and supervised-housing conditions: a co-op purchase is not an ordinary rental. Do not announce that every application, board, transfer or move-related charge has a universal $20 ceiling. [Current RPL § 238-a](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/238-A).

For ordinary rentals, the NY Attorney General explains the one-month security limit and that landlords cannot demand both last month's rent and a security deposit. [AG tenant guidance](https://ag.ny.gov/resources/individuals/tenants-homeowners/tenants). GOL § 7-108 has specific regulated-housing, seasonal-unit and owner-occupied co-op distinctions; its co-op exception requires the applicant to be the owner/purchaser/shareholder with exclusive occupancy under a proprietary lease. That does not mean every co-op sublet or condominium rental is exempt. [GOL § 7-108](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GOB/7-108).

**Review before publication:** classify the payer, recipient, transaction (ordinary rental versus ownership), board role and legal authority for each fee. Label unconfirmed board/move charges “Not yet confirmed”; do not present them as automatically lawful. Condo board charges and co-op sublet charges need specific review, not a blanket building-type exemption.

## Pets, service animals and emotional support animals

In NYC, an emotional support animal helps alleviate disability effects and need not have task training; a service animal performs trained disability-related work/tasks. Neither should be treated as an ordinary pet for accommodation purposes. CCHR says housing providers must permit ESAs as a reasonable accommodation absent undue hardship, with limits for prohibited animals or actual disruptive/damaging conduct. Ordinary pet bans, breed/weight restrictions and extra pet deposits/fees do not automatically apply. Where disability/need is not apparent, limited confirmation may be requested; a diagnosis and rigid documentation format cannot be required. [CCHR housing-animal FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/cchr/downloads/pdf/materials/EmotionalSupportAnimals_Housing_FactSheet.pdf).

Covered housing providers must conduct a timely cooperative dialogue and conclude it with a written determination identifying accommodations granted or denied. [CCHR disability enforcement guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/site/cchr/law/disability-discrimination-legal-guidance.page).

**Important federal update:** HUD withdrew FHEO Notices 2013-01 and 2020-01 effective September 17, 2025; its April 6, 2026 official notice says those documents should not be relied on as authoritative. Guidance withdrawal is not repeal of NYC protections or all disability-accommodation law. A national rollout must separately review current federal/state/local standards; do not reuse HUD's 2020 animal guidance as current nationwide authority. [HUD official withdrawal notice](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-06624.pdf).

**Proposed wording:** “Pet policy: [verified policy]. Disability-related accommodation requests are considered separately.” Provide “Request an accommodation” privately, with a named responsible contact and minimal information. Do not demand disability documentation in the public inquiry form, or hide a listing solely because the consumer indicates an assistance animal. Consumer explanations should distinguish a request and dialogue from a guaranteed outcome.

## Additional connection-stage checkpoint

NYC fair-chance housing provisions constrain criminal-history inquiry, background-check timing and adverse decisions. Section 8-107(5)(o) generally places a compliant conditional housing commitment and notice before a criminal check, with individualized assessment, information rights and exceptions. The detailed screening workflow needs its own review; the search inquiry should not ask criminal-history questions. [NYC Human Rights Law](https://www.nyc.gov/site/cchr/law/chapter-1.page).

## Rights and responsibilities must be usable

Recommended consumer actions: “Understand rental costs,” “Ask about application requirements,” “Request an accommodation,” and “Report a concern.” Link source-income/disability concerns directly to [CCHR](https://www.nyc.gov/site/cchr/index.page), fees to [DCWP's complaint guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/FAQ-Broker-Fees.page), and deposit issues to [NY Attorney General tenant resources](https://ag.ny.gov/resources/individuals/tenants-homeowners/tenants). An internal report must not be described as filing an official complaint. Preserve relevant listing versions, timestamps, communications and responsibility assignments as proposed operational policy; protect sensitive accommodation and assistance information from public marketing and ranking.
